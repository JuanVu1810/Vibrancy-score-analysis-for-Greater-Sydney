Greater Sydney vibrancy index

{{TITLE}}

This page ranks the areas of Greater Sydney by how many of the conditions for lively street life they have: people living nearby, businesses, public transport, a mix of activities and a connected street network. We call the result a vibrancy score. It is a screening tool that points to places worth a closer look, and it does not count how many people are actually on the street.

We scored {{SCORED_SA2S}} of {{TOTAL_SA2S}} areas (called SA2s). The score is standardised, so 100 is the Greater Sydney average and 10 points is one standard deviation, a typical spread. If you rank every area and split the list into five equal groups, each group is a quintile. The top quintile takes up {{TOP_AREA}} of the land we scored, holds {{TOP_RESIDENTS}} of residents, and is concentrated around the inner city and major centres.

What this page shows

These are the ideas the page relies on. Every chart below also has a short "How to read it" note, and a glossary near the end explains the technical terms.

  • Vibrancy. Here it means the conditions that make lively, varied and walkable street life possible: people living nearby, businesses and services, public transport, a mix of uses and a connected street network. It is a proxy, which means a stand-in. It is not a count of how busy the streets are.
  • SA2. A statistical area drawn by the Australian Bureau of Statistics. An SA2 is usually a suburb or a few suburbs joined together, and Greater Sydney has {{TOTAL_SA2S}}. Some large outer SA2s are mostly bushland or farmland.
  • The score. For each SA2 we worked out 14 measures (called indicators), put them on a common scale, averaged them within three groups (called pillars) and then averaged the three pillars. A score of 100 is the Greater Sydney average, and 110 is one typical spread above it. The score only means something compared with other SA2s: it is not a percentage, and 100 is not a pass mark.
  • The three pillars. Intensity is how much is packed into an area: residents, businesses, shops, stops and services for each square kilometre. Diversity is how varied the mix of businesses and land uses is. Design is how connected and easy to cross the streets are, using street intersections and pedestrian crossings.
  • Rank and quintile. Rank 1 is the highest-scoring SA2 and rank {{SCORED_SA2S}} is the lowest. A quintile is one fifth of the ranked list, so the top quintile is the highest-scoring fifth.
  • How to use the page. Start with the map: click any area to open its card, or use the buttons to switch what the map shows. Each chart below it asks one question and gives the answer in its heading. How it was made explains how the score was built, Limitations says what it cannot tell you, Next steps says what could improve it, and a table at the bottom lets you sort every score and download it.

Please read the Limitations section before using the ranking for any decision. In short, the score describes conditions, not crowds, and individual ranks move when the method changes.

Where is it most vibrant?

Vibrancy is highest in the inner city, and big centres like Parramatta and Liverpool stand out

The map shades every SA2 by its quintile: the darker the colour, the higher the SA2 sits in the ranking. Nearby SA2s often have similar scores (global Moran’s I {{GLOBAL_I}}, p = {{GLOBAL_P}}), so high-scoring areas tend to sit together instead of being scattered. The p-value comes from 999 random shuffles of the scores, so the smallest value it can show is 0.001.

How to read it: Colours show quintiles of the ranking, and the legend gives each quintile's score range. {{SCORE_MIDDLE_SHARE}} of SA2s score between 90 and 110, so most places are close to the average and the gaps between neighbouring ranks are often small. Click or tap an area to open its card, or type a name in the search box. The buttons above the map change what it shows:

  • Overall score: the final score, in quintiles.
  • Intensity, Diversity and Design: one pillar at a time, each with its own quintiles.
  • Place types: four kinds of place, from whether an area is dense and whether it is diverse.
  • Hot spots: groups of neighbouring SA2s with similar scores. These are descriptive, and the Limitations section explains why.

The buttons under those jump the map to Greater Sydney, the inner city, Parramatta or Liverpool.

What if the pillars counted differently?

The overall score gives Intensity, Diversity and Design the same weight. That is a choice, not a fact, so you can change it here. Drag a slider to make a pillar count for more or less, or pick a preset, and watch the map and the ranks move. This is a way to see how much the ranking depends on that choice, and there is no single right answer. Weights affect the Overall score only. The pillar layers, place types and hot spots stay as they are in the baseline, which is the main version of the score with equal weights.

How was it made?

How the data and score were built

{{METHODS_DETAIL}}

Why "density" has two formulas

The word appears twice in these methods, for two different jobs. Only the first one goes into the score.

  • Per-area value (step 2): used to calculate the score. The count of something divided by the area of the SA2, such as stops per square kilometre. The 12 per-area indicators are the raw inputs that become the Intensity and Design pillars.
  • Density score (step 4): used to describe the results. The average of the finished Intensity and Design scores. It is worked out after the score exists, only to sort SA2s into dense or less dense for the place types and the chart. It is not an input to the score, and it does not change any score or rank.

Open any step below for the detail. The limits of these methods are listed in the Limitations section after it.

1. Prepare and check the data15 public sources cleaned, checked and placed into SA2s
  • Python cleaned and checked 15 public sources and loaded 17 data tables into PostgreSQL/PostGIS, a database that can store map data. Two more tables record where each source came from and the results of the quality checks, making 19 tables in all.
  • Every downloaded file is checked against a recorded checksum, so a file that has changed is caught before it is used.
  • SQL and GeoPandas (a Python mapping library) put each located item, such as a bus stop or a pedestrian crossing, into the SA2 that contains it. A point exactly on a boundary goes to the SA2 with the lower code, so it is counted once.
  • Records with a missing location, and records outside Greater Sydney, were counted as each indicator was built. For every source, the number that came in equals the number assigned plus the number dropped, and the assigned totals were checked against the totals expected from the data, so the exclusions can be checked.
2. Measure 14 things in every SA2Ten for Intensity, two for Diversity and two for Design

An indicator is one measurable ingredient of street life. Most are counts for each square kilometre of the whole SA2, so a large SA2 is not given a high score just for being large.

per-area value = number of items in the SA2 ÷ area of the SA2 (km²)

Residents are counted per hectare instead: residents ÷ (area in km² × 100). The 12 indicators measured this way are called the per-area indicators below. This is a different idea from the "density" used for place types, which is explained in step 4.

The two mix indicators use normalised entropy, which measures how evenly something is spread:

H = − Σi pi · ln(pi) ÷ ln(K)

pi is the share of the SA2's businesses (or land) that falls in category i, and K is the number of categories: 19 industries or 5 land uses. Categories with no businesses or land are left out of the sum. H is 0 when everything is in one category and 1 when it is spread evenly.

3. Turn the indicators into a scoreSix steps, from the 14 measures to a ranked score
  1. Choose which SA2s get a score. Centennial Park has no residents and no registered businesses, so it is left out and the other {{SCORED_SA2S}} are scored.
  2. Shrink the extremes. The 12 per-area indicators are log-transformed first. Without that, a few SA2s with very large counts would dominate. After it, the gap between 10 and 100 for each square kilometre counts for about as much as the gap between 100 and 1,000. The two mix indicators already run from 0 to 1, so they are not transformed. x′ = ln(1 + x)
  3. Put every indicator on the same scale. Counts for each square kilometre and mix measures use different units, so each indicator is converted to a z-score: how many typical spreads (standard deviations) an SA2 sits above or below the average of the scored SA2s. z = (x − μ) ÷ σ μ is the average and σ the standard deviation of the indicator across the scored SA2s (dividing by the number of SA2s). Missing values are ignored.
  4. Average within each pillar, then across pillars. The three pillars get equal weight. An SA2 that is missing a pillar is scored on the pillars it has. Intensity = average of its 10 z values Diversity = average of its 2 z values    Design = average of its 2 z values V = (Intensity + Diversity + Design) ÷ 3 Only the values and pillars that exist are averaged. Because Intensity has ten indicators and the others two, each Diversity or Design indicator carries more weight than each Intensity indicator.
  5. Rescale. The final number is rescaled so that 100 is the Greater Sydney average and 10 points is one standard deviation. {{SCORE_MIDDLE_SHARE}} of the scored SA2s sit between 90 and 110. Score = 100 + 10 × (V − average of V) ÷ standard deviation of V The average and standard deviation are taken across the scored SA2s. Each pillar score shown on the page is rescaled the same way from its own average.
  6. Rank and group. The SA2s are ranked from 1 (highest) and split into five quintiles of 74 or 75 SA2s each. quintile = 5 − ⌊(rank − 1) × 5 ÷ number of scored SA2s⌋ Quintile 5 is the top fifth (ranks 1 to 75) and quintile 1 is the bottom fifth.
4. Check the resultStability, neighbours, place types, income and walking counts
  • Stability. The score was rebuilt 22 ways and the ranks were compared. Each version changes one thing: dropping one data source (12 versions), skipping the log transform, scaling from 0 to 1 instead of with z-scores, weighting every indicator equally, using Intensity alone, using a three-indicator benchmark (residents, land-use mix and street intersections), counting the number of industries instead of how evenly they are spread, leaving out the four industries that are mostly home-based, and three other ways of giving school catchments to SA2s. rank range = worst rank − best rank, across the baseline and the 22 versions Spearman correlation = ordinary correlation between the two lists of ranks
  • Neighbours. Moran's I, a standard measure, checks whether nearby SA2s score alike, and a local version marks groups of similar neighbours as hot spots and cold spots. global I = Σi zi · (Σj wij zj) ÷ Σi zi² local Ii = zi × (average z of the neighbours of i) p = (1 + shuffles with |I shuffled| ≥ |I observed|) ÷ (1 + 999) zi is SA2 i's standardised score, and wij is a weight that is the same for every neighbour of i and zero for other SA2s, so that the weights of each SA2 add up to 1. Neighbours are SA2s that share a border or corner. The average of the local values equals the global I. The local test shuffles the other SA2s' scores 999 times. An SA2 is labelled when p < 0.05: high-high or low-low when its own score and its neighbours' average are both above or both below the average, and an outlier when they differ.
  • Place types. SA2s were sorted by whether they are dense and whether they are diverse. Here "density" is a score made from the Intensity and Design pillars. It is not the count per square kilometre used for the individual indicators. density score = (Intensity score + Design score) ÷ 2 dense if density score ≥ 100    diverse if Diversity score ≥ 100 These are the pillar scores shown on the page, where 100 is the Greater Sydney average. The chart in the place-type section draws its lines at the same 100.
  • Income. SA2s were grouped by median income to see who lives in the highest-scoring places. share = residents in top-quintile SA2s of the group ÷ all residents of the group The three groups are cut at the points that put about a third of residents in each.
  • Walking counts. Scores were compared with real pedestrian counts for the 16 inner-city SA2s that have them. For each site we averaged the four most recent surveys (October 2024 to March 2026), then averaged the sites in each SA2.

Source dates: 2021 to 2026, so the inputs are not all from the same year. Data credits: ABS, Transport for NSW, NSW Education, AEC, NSW Spatial Services, City of Sydney (walking counts, used only to check the score) and OpenStreetMap. Contains OpenStreetMap data (c) OpenStreetMap contributors.

What are the limits?

Limitations: a rough guide to where street life is possible, not a count of people

Read this before using the ranking. It says what the score can and cannot tell you. The ideas that need a picture come first, then the limits in the data and in the methods, and a short glossary closes the section.

What the score is good for

  • Spotting areas that have the ingredients for lively streets: homes, businesses, transport and a connected street network.
  • Comparing parts of Greater Sydney with each other.
  • Choosing where to look more closely.

What it cannot tell you

  • How many people are actually in an area at any time.
  • An exact rank: the median SA2 moves {{MEDIAN_RANGE}} places when one choice in the method changes.
  • Whether an area is a better place to live.
  • Why an area scores the way it does. Nothing here shows cause.

Each square is one scored SA2, in rank order: rank 1 is the top left square and the last rank is the bottom right. The marked, high-contrast squares are the SA2s where the City of Sydney counts people walking. They all sit at the very start of the list, so we could not test whether the score works for the middle or the bottom. Where we could test, the link was weak (Spearman {{WEEKDAY_CORRELATION}} on weekdays and {{WEEKEND_CORRELATION}} on weekends, where 0 means no link and 1 means the same order).

Some clusters on the map could be due to chance

The cluster outlines come from a test run on each SA2 in turn. A test like this is set so that, when there is no real pattern, about 1 time in 20 it still looks special. Run it on every SA2 and about {{BY_CHANCE}} could look special by luck alone. The standard fix, called a false discovery rate correction, has not been applied here, so treat the clusters as a description of the map, not as proven hot spots or cold spots. Because neighbouring SA2s are not independent, {{BY_CHANCE}} is only a rough guide. In this run {{LABELLED_CLUSTERS}} SA2s were labelled, far more than {{BY_CHANCE}}, so many of the labels are probably real, but we cannot tell which ones.

Limits in the data

    {{LIMITS}}

Limits in the methods

  • Equal weights are a choice. Intensity has ten indicators, while Diversity and Design have two each, so each Diversity or Design indicator counts for far more than each Intensity indicator. The 22 alternative versions and the weight sliders show how much the ranking depends on this, but none of them says which weights are right.
  • The score is relative. The scaling uses only the scored SA2s, so 100 means the Greater Sydney average. A score cannot be compared with another city or another year.
  • Areas are the unit. Counts are divided by the area of the whole SA2, so a large SA2 with a busy town inside it can still score low. A different boundary system could give different scores.
  • Missing pillars. An SA2 missing a pillar is scored on the pillars it has, so four SA2s without a Diversity pillar are not scored on exactly the same basis as the others.
  • The cluster labels are not corrected for testing many areas. An SA2 is labelled a cluster when its local Moran's I p-value is below 0.05. The test uses neighbours that share a border or corner, with 999 random shuffles per SA2, so the smallest p-value is 0.001. No false discovery rate (FDR) or Bonferroni correction is applied across the 372 tests, so about 19 tests would pass by chance alone. That figure is only a rough guide, because neighbouring tests are not independent. {{LABELLED_CLUSTERS}} SA2s passed, far more than 19, so many labels are probably real, but we cannot tell which ones.
  • The clusters are descriptive. They are not confirmed hot spots or cold spots. The global Moran's I of 0.55 says that nearby scores are alike, not why.
  • Place types use a sharp cut. An SA2 is dense or diverse when its score is at or above the Greater Sydney average, so SA2s close to that line can switch type with a small change.
  • The checks are modest. The 22 alternative versions change the choices one at a time. They do not give a confidence interval for any SA2's score. The walking-count check has only 16 SA2s, all in the top quintile, and the income comparison uses area medians, not households.

No claim here is causal. The score describes where conditions for street activity are present, and it does not explain why they are.

Glossary
SA2
A statistical area of the Australian Bureau of Statistics, usually a suburb or a few suburbs. Greater Sydney has 373.
Proxy
A stand-in. The score measures conditions that allow street activity, as a stand-in for activity itself.
Indicator and pillar
An indicator is one measure, such as transport stops per square kilometre. A pillar groups indicators: Intensity, Diversity and Design.
Quintile
One fifth of the ranked list. The top quintile is the highest-scoring fifth of SA2s.
Standardised score
A score rescaled so that 100 is the Greater Sydney average and 10 points is a typical spread (one standard deviation).
Log transform
A way of shrinking very large values, so that a few extreme areas do not swamp everyone else.
Moran's I
A number that says whether nearby areas have similar scores. Near 0 means no pattern, and closer to 1 means neighbours tend to look alike.
Cluster, hot spot and cold spot
A group of neighbouring SA2s with similar scores. A hot spot is high among high, and a cold spot is low among low.
False discovery rate
A correction for running many tests at once, which makes it harder for chance results to look real.
Spearman correlation
How closely two lists put things in the same order: 0 means no link and 1 means the same order.
Density (place types)
The average of an SA2's Intensity and Design scores. An SA2 is "dense" when this is at or above 100. It is a different idea from the counts per square kilometre used for the individual indicators.
Baseline
The main version of the score, built as described in How it was made, with equal pillar weights. The 22 alternative versions and the weight sliders are compared with it.
High-high, low-low and outliers
Labels for an SA2 and its neighbours. High-high means a high score among high-scoring neighbours, and low-low means a low score among low-scoring neighbours. A high-low or low-high outlier is an SA2 that differs from its neighbours.
Median
The middle value when everything is lined up from smallest to largest. Half the values are below it and half are above.
Alternative versions
The 22 ways of rebuilding the score with one choice changed, such as dropping an indicator, used to see how much the ranking depends on that choice.

What could come next?

Next steps: each one answers a limitation above

These are ideas to test, and we do not know yet whether they would change the ranking.

  • Registered businesses are mostly home-based. Try shopfront or street-level data, for example from OpenStreetMap or council records, and see whether the ranking changes.
  • There are no jobs, floor-area or building-age data. Add workplace job counts and floor-area or building-age data where they exist for all of Greater Sydney.
  • The inputs come from different dates. Rebuild the index for one reference year, or refresh the sources together.
  • SA2s differ a lot in size. Repeat the score on a finer or more even set of areas, such as a grid or mesh blocks, to see how much the boundaries matter.
  • Walking counts exist only for the inner city. Collect or request counts from outer and middle-ranked areas, so the check also covers the middle and the bottom of the ranking.
  • Cluster labels are not corrected for many tests. Add a false discovery rate correction to the local Moran's I results and see which of the high-high and low-low SA2s remain.
  • Weights are equal by choice. Test weights set by experts, or weights learned from measured activity, and compare them with the equal-weight ranking.
  • Individual ranks are unstable. Report each SA2 as a rank range or a score band, not a single rank.
